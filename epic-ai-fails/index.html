Epic AI Fails

The polished demo is rarely the interesting part. I keep notes on the moments when AI, automation, permissions, agents, and governance collide with reality. The useful question is not “did it fail?” It is “what operating assumption failed with it?”

Fail 01

7/10Failed successfully

The Hallmark enterprise software avatar

I asked an AI avatar tool to create a version of me. It created a woman who looked like she might sell enterprise software on the Hallmark Channel.

She was polished. She was plausible. She was not me.

Sometimes generic AI feels less artificial than personalized AI.

The sane version

Use synthetic presenters when the presenter does not need to be you. If identity matters, require human review for voice, mannerisms, appearance, and whether the result actually represents the person rather than a statistically attractive approximation.

Fail 02

10/10Governance theater

The mission-critical workflow nobody used

At a SaaS company, leaders became concerned that disabling an AI integration could break workflows people depended on. Multiple teams got involved. Cost controls were discussed. The vendor was pulled in. The workflow had become “critical” through repetition.

Then somebody checked the usage data.

The workflow was so critical that it had been run zero times.

0actual uses
Severalteams involved
1missing first question

The problem was not the integration. The problem was treating availability as dependency before verifying adoption.

The sane version

  1. Confirm the business owner and intended use case.
  2. Check actual usage before escalating availability risk.
  3. Define cost, access, and approval controls before broad enablement.
  4. Test with a controlled group.
  5. Expand only when usage and value justify the risk.

Fail 03

10/10Agentic limbo

My AI worked all night. Except it didn’t.

An agentic website task appeared to keep working for roughly nine hours. It had completed substantial work, hit a publishing handoff problem, launched monitoring agents, and then stopped making forward progress.

The monitoring agents finished. The parent workflow never reconciled that fact. The interface kept displaying activity.

“Still working” became a very polished way of saying “stuck.”

~9 hrsapparently working
0active subagents at discovery
0verified deployments

The failure was operational, not magical: no effective timeout, bounded retry, durable recovery path, or final reconciliation step asking whether the requested business outcome had actually happened.

The sane version

  1. Define the exact completion event.
  2. Set a maximum wait for every external handoff.
  3. Use a heartbeat that proves forward progress, not just activity.
  4. Bound retries.
  5. Escalate blockers clearly.
  6. Checkpoint durable state.
  7. Finish with one question: did the requested outcome actually occur?

Fail 04

10/10Shadow automation

The automation that got automation banned

Leadership wanted us to move faster on AI and automation.

Then Salesforce started throwing 500+ flow errors.

We spent about a day cleaning it up and eventually found the source: a certified Salesforce admin from another department had been given production access and had quietly started deploying changes through n8n.

Her manager didn’t know. The executive who supported the access didn’t know. We definitely didn’t know.

But now leadership knew one thing: n8n bad.

500+flow errors
~1 daycleanup
0owners informed

So the team actually responsible for automation became more constrained.

Then came the obvious follow-up: Why aren’t the AI automations moving faster?

Automate faster → shadow automation breaks production → become afraid of automation → slow governed automation → demand faster automation.

n8n got convicted for a crime committed by governance.

The lesson

A Salesforce certification is not a change-management process. And neither is executive sponsorship.

Automated Beyond Recognition

The recurring pattern deserves its own fictional scorecard: take a simple business problem, solve it with as many tools, nodes, agents, alerts, and failure points as financially possible, then discover the last step is still “send Jess a Slack message so she can fix Salesforce manually.”

The machine’s only output is a more expensive to-do list for a human.

That joke lands because the operating mistake is real: companies often automate the movement and notification of bad data without automating the decision or correction.

Why keep the failures?

Because failure exposes architecture. A bad output may be a model problem. A stalled workflow may be an orchestration problem. A “critical” integration nobody uses is a governance problem. The fix depends on naming the right layer instead of adding another tool.
